# Case Reports Assessed Using the WHO-UMC Scale and Naranjo's Adverse Drug Reaction Probability Scale

## Case 1: Tacrolimus-Induced Thrombotic Microangiopathy in a Kidney Transplant Recipient

**Patient:** 48-year-old kidney transplant recipient.
**Suspected drug:** Tacrolimus.
**Indication:** Initiated for treatment of acute T-cell-mediated allograft rejection.
**Adverse reaction:** Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (schistocytes on peripheral smear, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, with associated neurologic and gastrointestinal symptoms.
**Source:** Liao YH, Huang MY. *American Journal of Case Reports*. 2026 Jul 2;27:e953247. doi:10.12659/AJCR.953247. (Open access.)

Twenty-three days after starting tacrolimus for acute allograft rejection, the patient developed anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia was confirmed on peripheral smear and laboratory testing, and gastrointestinal bleeding was excluded. A comprehensive work-up — ADAMTS13 activity, complement studies, coagulation testing, and infectious screening — excluded alternative causes of TMA. Although tacrolimus trough levels remained within the therapeutic range, the temporal relationship supported tacrolimus-induced TMA. Tacrolimus was discontinued, with complete haematologic and renal recovery within 10 days (positive dechallenge). On follow-up, tacrolimus was reintroduced for refractory rejection, and the haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia recurred (positive rechallenge), again resolving after the drug was withdrawn.

### WHO-UMC Causality Assessment

| WHO-UMC Criterion | Assessment in This Case |
|---|---|
| Time relationship | Plausible — onset 23 days after starting tacrolimus, consistent with drug-induced TMA. |
| Alternative explanations | Systematically excluded — ADAMTS13 activity, complement studies, coagulation work-up, and infectious screening ruled out other causes of TMA; gastrointestinal bleeding was excluded as a cause of the anaemia. |
| Dechallenge | Positive — complete haematologic and renal recovery within 10 days of stopping tacrolimus. |
| Rechallenge | Positive — haemolytic anaemia and thrombocytopenia recurred on reintroduction of tacrolimus for refractory rejection, and resolved again on withdrawal. |
| Pharmacological/phenomenological plausibility | High — tacrolimus is a recognised cause of TMA in solid-organ transplant recipients, with a reported incidence of 1–4.7%. |

**WHO-UMC Classification: Certain**

This concordance is expected: a positive rechallenge is the single most decisive piece of evidence in either system, and it is present and well documented here.

**FINAL CLASSIFICATION: DEFINITE / CERTAIN**

## Case 2: Stevens-Johnson Syndrome / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis Overlap Following Ceftriaxone

**Patient:** 32-year-old female.
**Suspected drug:** Ceftriaxone 1 g, intravenous.
**Indication:** Fever, cough, and expectoration (presumed respiratory infection).
**Adverse reaction:** SJS/TEN overlap — generalised rash with atypical target lesions, positive Nikolsky's sign, ocular congestion, and mucosal erosions.
**Source:** Prasad RV, Kumar V A, Kalaiselvan V, et al. *Cureus*. 2026 Jul 10;18(7):e112392. doi:10.7759/cureus.112392. (Open access.)

Following intravenous ceftriaxone, the patient developed a generalised rash with peri-oral, truncal, and lower-limb involvement, eye congestion, and a positive Nikolsky's sign, leading to a clinical diagnosis of SJS/TEN overlap. Ceftriaxone was withdrawn immediately and multidisciplinary supportive care was instituted, including systemic corticosteroids, ocular and mucosal care, and nutritional support. The patient recovered over approximately one month. Rechallenge was not performed, as it would be contraindicated given the severity of the reaction.

### WHO-UMC Causality Assessment

| WHO-UMC Criterion | Assessment in This Case |
|---|---|
| Time relationship | Plausible — the reaction developed shortly after ceftriaxone administration, within the known onset window for antibiotic-induced SJS/TEN. |
| Alternative explanations | The underlying febrile illness is a recognised trigger of SJS/TEN in its own right and cannot be entirely excluded, though no other new drug was introduced before onset. |
| Dechallenge | Positive — clinical improvement followed withdrawal of ceftriaxone and supportive treatment. |
| Rechallenge | Not performed and not ethically appropriate given the severity and potential lethality of SJS/TEN. |
| Pharmacological/phenomenological plausibility | High — cephalosporins, including ceftriaxone, are a well-established cause of SJS/TEN. |

**WHO-UMC Classification: Probable / Likely**

**FINAL CLASSIFICATION: PROBABLE**

## Case 3: Hydroxychloroquine-Induced Renal Phospholipidosis with Proximal Tubulopathy

**Patient:** 36-year-old woman with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).
**Suspected drug:** Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), continuous therapy for approximately 18 months.
**Indication:** Systemic lupus erythematosus.
**Adverse reaction:** Slowly progressive kidney dysfunction with Fanconi syndrome (normoglycaemic glycosuria, pan-aminoaciduria, hypophosphataemia, hypouricaemia, metabolic acidosis) and biopsy-proven renal phospholipidosis (glomerular "zebra," "myeloid," and "curvilinear" bodies, with lysosomal inclusions also in proximal tubular epithelial cells).
**Source:** Manabe S, Seki M, et al.; Hoshino J. *BMC Nephrology*. 2026 May 4;27(1):379. doi:10.1186/s12882-026-05021-w. (Open access.)

After approximately 18 months of hydroxychloroquine therapy for SLE, the patient developed slowly progressive kidney dysfunction (eGFR decline of −11.2 mL/min/1.73 m²/year) with minimal proteinuria and no active urinary sediment to suggest a lupus nephritis flare, but with marked tubular injury markers and a distinct Fanconi syndrome. Renal biopsy demonstrated characteristic phospholipidosis on electron microscopy. Extensive clinical, biochemical, genetic, and histological evaluation excluded Fabry disease, and immunofluorescence did not support an active lupus nephritis process. The published report does not describe the clinical course after hydroxychloroquine withdrawal.

### WHO-UMC Causality Assessment

| WHO-UMC Criterion | Assessment in This Case |
|---|---|
| Time relationship | Plausible for a cumulative-exposure mechanism — drug-induced phospholipidosis typically develops over months to years of therapy, consistent with 18 months of exposure here. |
| Alternative explanations | Fabry disease was excluded by genetic/enzymatic testing, and active lupus nephritis was made unlikely by the absence of active sediment and negative immunofluorescence for that process. |
| Dechallenge | Not reported — the published case does not document the clinical or biochemical course after HCQ withdrawal. |
| Rechallenge | Not performed / not reported. |
| Pharmacological/phenomenological plausibility | High — HCQ-induced renal phospholipidosis is mechanistically well described, and the biopsy findings are a recognised morphological signature of this drug class. |

**WHO-UMC Classification: Possible**

Although the temporal relationship is plausible and the principal alternative diagnosis (Fabry disease) was systematically excluded, the WHO-UMC scale requires information on the response to drug withdrawal for its Probable or Certain categories; since dechallenge outcome is not reported, the classification defaults to Possible. On Naranjo scoring the case reaches only the lower boundary of the Probable band (score 6) despite strong histological and mechanistic support, again reflecting the missing dechallenge/rechallenge items. The final classification adopted here is the more conservative of the two: Possible, pending follow-up data on the response to withdrawal.
